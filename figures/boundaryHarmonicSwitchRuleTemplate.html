The Latching Switch

What the tissue does between releasing the boundary clamp and showing a face, reduced to one rule and four phases. Every cell is a switch with a memory; its switching window slides when its neighbours change state; and a conductance driven by the tissue's own field sweeps the whole population across that window twice. The rule is derived from the model's constants, not fitted, and it accounts for 99.1–99.6% of every cell's state at every iteration across all seven trained codes. Applied to two ensembles of 1,024 codes, it says where the face is won: in one phase, by two numbers, which the code's orders steer — separably near a program that works, not across the space as a whole.

The rule

One cell, two thresholds, and a memory in between

A cell's voltage settles far faster than its polarising conductance G changes, so at every moment the cell rests at a stable root of its own voltage equation. Solving that equation with the cell's neighbours held where they are gives two critical conductances. Above G_up the depolarised root stops existing and the cell has nowhere to be but dark. Below G_down the hyperpolarised root stops existing and it has nowhere to be but light. Between them both roots exist, and the cell simply keeps whichever one it is already on. That middle band is the memory.

The thresholds are not fixed. Each neighbour pulls on the cell through its gap junctions, so a light neighbour lifts both thresholds by about 0.036 and a dark neighbour lowers them by about 0.032. A cell that goes dark therefore makes it easier for the cells beside it to go dark too. That single fact is what turns a conductance map into a sculpted pattern.

The trajectory

The conductance sweeps the window twice

With the rule fixed, everything else is the story of one number per cell. The clamp drives G up across the whole tissue; releasing it lets G collapse through the bottom of the window, which erases what the hold wrote; then the field the tissue itself generates drives G up a second time — and this rise is selective, lifting the future feature cells well clear of the rest. The face is written on that second rise and latched as G falls back.

The choreography

Nucleate, then recruit

On the second rise the cells do not cross together. A few reach G_up on their own — they need a high conductance, because with every neighbour still light their threshold is at its highest. The rest cross later at a conductance that would not have been enough earlier, because by then some neighbour has gone dark and pulled their threshold down. Call the first kind nucleators and the second recruits: the split is a statement about the threshold each cell faced, and it is what gives the pattern its shape rather than just its extent.

It is tempting to read that as darkness spreading outward from seed cells, one block setting off the next. The causal test below says otherwise. The threshold shift between touching cells is real and it is in the rule, but when a cell is actually prevented from darkening, what changes is not mostly its neighbours.

Across the code space

What the orders actually steer

The same reduction applied to two ensembles of 1,024 codes each: one scattered across the whole feasible region, one drawn within ±0.10 of the trained code. Every code runs the same four phases — the structure is universal. What differs is the writing in phase 3, and that is where the face is won or lost.

Two numbers describe it. Selectivity is how far the second rise lifts the face's cells above the rest. Targeting is whether the cells it lifts highest are the right ones — of the fourteen highest-conductance interior cells at the second peak, how many belong to the face. Scored at each code's own best moment rather than at a fixed iteration, these two predict the face a run reaches at R² = 0.67 near a working code.

The division of labour between the orders appears only near a working code. Scattered across the whole space, a₀ dominates almost everything and the orders do not separate — which is what the earlier knockout test found at the level of the pattern. Move to the neighbourhood of a code that works and the roles come apart cleanly.

The causal test

Hold one cell's conductance and watch what follows

Everything so far describes the runs. To show the rule is a cause rather than a correlate, one cell's conductance is overridden during the selective rise, iterations 1,250–2,173, in an otherwise untouched run of the trained code. Predictions were fixed before the runs.

How well it holds

Where the rule is right, and where it is only nearly right

The test is one step ahead: given the tissue's true state, does the rule say what each cell does next? Across all seven trained codes it is right for 99.1% to 99.6% of cell-moments. The residual is not a missing mechanism — it is timing jitter at the crossing itself.

What this does not do. Run open-loop from the release, the reduced model drifts: it recovers the final pattern to about 79 of 81 interior cells, but not every event, because a threshold missed by a hair changes a neighbour count and the error compounds in a system already known to be extremely sensitive. The rule is a statement about each transition given the true state, not a claim that two thousand iterations can be replayed from bits alone.

The conductance itself is taken as given here. It is driven by the extracellular field, which the tissue's own voltage generates — so the second rise's selectivity is inherited from a feedback loop this page does not open. That loop is the remaining question, not a solved one.
